Is a Healthy Diet Affordable?

Key message: Global affordability improved despite rising costs. Progress was uneven, with Sub-Saharan Africa moving in the opposite direction. Prices need to be assessed alongside purchasing power and nutrition outcomes. The economic overview uses 2025; the nutrition comparison uses 2024, the latest year with nutrition data.

1. Requirements and analyst responsibilities

2. Four key insights

Insight 1 — Global affordability improved as costs rose

The population-weighted cost of a healthy diet rose from 2.94 to 4.28 PPP dollars per person per day between 2017 and 2025, an increase of 45.6%. Over the same period, the share of people unable to afford it fell from 41.0% to 32.7%, a decrease of 8.3 percentage points. The number affected fell from 3,135.5 million to 2,692.1 million, a reduction of 443.4 million. Progress reversed temporarily in 2020, when the rate rose from 38.0% in 2019 to 39.6%.

Interpretation: Price trends alone do not explain affordability. These descriptive results do not establish what caused the improvement. Changes in current PPP costs are also not a pure measure of real food-price inflation.

Insight 2 — Sub-Saharan Africa is falling behind

In Sub-Saharan Africa, unaffordability increased from 70.3% to 72.2% (+1.9 percentage points). The number affected rose from 759.6 million to 953.9 million (+194.3 million). The region accounted for approximately 35.4% of the global affected population in 2025. By contrast, South Asia recorded a decline of 23.7 percentage points and 330.6 million people. Middle East & North Africa also deteriorated, with increases of 3.0 percentage points and 65.6 million people.

Interpretation: Global progress can conceal regional setbacks. Regional charts should use the publisher's aggregates directly.

Insight 3 — Affordability is strongly associated with income

In 2025, the population-weighted average diet cost in low-income economies was 4.05 PPP dollars per day, close to the 4.20 recorded in high-income economies. Yet unaffordability was 76.1% versus 8.1%, a gap of 68.0 percentage points. Across 142 countries with both GDP per capita and unaffordability data, the Spearman correlation was −0.824. The correlation between diet cost and unaffordability was +0.172 across 147 countries (0.17 on the 142 countries that also have GDP data, the sample shown in the data story and dashboard).

Interpretation: The findings support assessing purchasing power alongside prices. The correlations are unweighted country-level comparisons, unlike the population-weighted aggregate costs. They use different sets of countries and are descriptive. Affordability is constructed using household income or expenditure, so an association with GDP per capita is partly expected and is not an independent causal test. GDP per capita is not household disposable income. These results do not establish causation or the effectiveness of any policy.

Insight 4 — Dietary energy access and healthy diet access are different

In Niger, 86.7% of the population could not afford a healthy diet in 2024, compared with a 12.9% prevalence of undernourishment (PoU) estimate for 2023–2025, stored under its midpoint year 2024. The pattern is stable: in 2022, 86.9% could not afford a healthy diet while PoU was 13.0% (2021–2023).

Across 125 countries in 2024 with both indicators, healthy diet unaffordability and stunting among children under five have a positive association: Spearman rho = 0.784 and Pearson r = 0.789. These statistics are unweighted country comparisons, not estimates of the relationship among individual children.

Interpretation: PoU measures insufficient habitual dietary energy. Healthy diet unaffordability measures economic access to a reference diet. Stunting measures low height for age in children under five. None directly measures individual nutrient intake or whether someone feels full. The Niger percentages are separate population-level measures: their difference is not a measured share of people who are adequately fed but nutrient deficient. Actual diet quality also depends on factors beyond affordability.

SDG link: PoU is indicator 2.1.1; child stunting is indicator 2.2.1, under target 2.2. This strengthens the nutrition narrative directly within SDG 2. Healthy diet affordability remains a complementary measure rather than an official substitute for either indicator.

Robustness: The positive association persists in 2022 and 2023 (Spearman 0.772 and 0.774, respectively; n = 125 each). Removing one country at a time leaves the 2024 Pearson correlation between 0.782 and 0.803. On the 123 countries also reporting GDP, the raw Pearson correlation is 0.787. After controlling for log GDP per capita in a simple linear residual comparison on those same countries, it falls to 0.411. This suggests shared socioeconomic conditions explain part of the raw relationship; the adjusted statistic still does not identify a causal effect. Child health, maternal nutrition, infection, sanitation and earlier-life conditions may also matter.

4. Data quality and limitations

  • The country file contains 1,620 rows: 180 countries or territories across nine years. ISO3–year keys are unique. The aggregate file contains 108 rows across 12 groups. The rates checked contain no negative values or values above 100.
  • For 2025, 167 countries have cost data, 147 have unaffordability rates and 165 have GDP data. Use complete cases for each analysis. Do not replace missing observations with zero.
  • Do not sum all aggregate rows. World, income and regional groups are overlapping classifications.
  • Country-level regions use the FY2027 classification, which differs from the FPN aggregate classification for MENA and South Asia. Filtering countries by region may therefore not reproduce the regional total. Agree on any reclassification with the Data Lead.
  • income_group uses the current classification for every year. The charts do not show historical movement between income groups.
  • PoU is a three-year average assigned to its middle year. Values reported as <2.5 are encoded as 2.5, an upper bound rather than an exact observation. Neither PoU nor stunting data are available for 2025. Do not silently join observations from different years. The nutrition panel uses 2024 affordability and stunting; PoU retains its centred 2023–2025 reporting window.
  • Food-group composition and energy-sufficient or nutrient-adequate diet costs are available only for 2021. Year filters must not imply that these fields cover 2017–2025.
  • 2024 nutrition coverage differs by outcome: 125 countries have both unaffordability and stunting, and 136 have both unaffordability and PoU. Of the 136 PoU pairs, 60 are censored below 2.5%. A sensitivity check excluding these gives Spearman rho = 0.688 across 76 countries, versus 0.806 with upper-bound coding. Exclusion changes the sample and is not a correction for censoring.
  • Stunting is a modelled estimate of a cumulative child growth outcome; same-year comparisons cannot establish timing or causation. The merged file contains no uncertainty intervals. Correlations use point estimates without propagating measurement uncertainty and may not represent countries with missing data. Repeated country-years are not pooled as independent observations.
  • PPP dollars are international purchasing-power dollars, rather than nominal US dollars. Affected population figures are already in millions. Changes in rates are reported in percentage points.
  • The two cleaned files were produced by the Data Lead from the original publisher downloads stored in raw/, and are checked by scripts/validate_data.py (24 checks, including every headline number). The dashboard copy of the data is verified identical to data/. coverage.csv, recalculated in this analysis, is an analyst output rather than part of the cleaned data.
  • The cleaning and merging scripts use relative paths: python scripts/clean_fpn.py, python scripts/merge_all.py and python scripts/validate_data.py rebuild and check the cleaned files from raw/. This analysis starts from the two cleaned CSV files.

5. Story description (final, as submitted)

The Food Atlas: The price of eating well

A healthy diet is getting more expensive – yet fewer people are priced out of it. Between 2017 and 2025, its daily cost rose from 2.94 to 4.28 PPP dollars per person, while the share unable to afford it fell from 41% to 33%.

But progress has left one region behind. Sub-Saharan Africa now has 194 million more people priced out than in 2017 – more than one in three of the world’s total.

The divide is income, not price. In 2025, a healthy diet cost 4.18 PPP dollars a day in both Nigeria and Cyprus, yet 80% of Nigerians could not afford it, against under 2% of Cypriots. On the plate, animal-source foods make up 38% of the cost in low-income countries, versus 24% in high-income ones (2021 country medians).

Being fed is not the same as being well fed. In Niger in 2024, 13% of people lacked enough calories, but 87% could not afford a healthy diet and nearly half of young children were stunted.

Our atlas traces this story from global prices to children’s growth, linking affordability to SDG targets 2.1 (ending hunger) and 2.2 (ending malnutrition).

198 words including the title. Every figure matches this analysis or the Data Lead’s validation checks: Nigeria and Cyprus 4.18 PPP dollars, 80.5% and 1.5%; animal-source food shares 38.1% (19 low-income countries) and 24.0% (62 high-income countries); Niger stunting 48.3% (2024).

6. Reproduction and validation

In RStudio, open the repository root and run source("analysis/analyst.R"). The script uses base R and exports tables to analysis/output/. Running it reproduces every table in this report and confirms all 17 headline figures (analysis/output/headline_checks.csv). analysis/verify_analysis.py recalculates the same statistics independently in Python and compares them with the R tables; both agree.
